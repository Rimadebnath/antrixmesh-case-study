    01 · The challenge

    What was asked kept changing, and the team stalled

    The client wanted a website that showed where in the world crypto news was happening. As the market boomed and new projects raised money by launching their own coins, the ask grew into something else entirely.

    01

    A small word with a big meaning

    The client asked to "make it Web3" midway through. To them it sounded like a tweak. Technically it meant wallet sign-in, smart contracts and a new architecture.

    02

    No shared language

    The client contact wasn't technical and often missed syncs. Our research and legal analysts spoke blockchain and compliance, the developers spoke React. Nobody was translating.

    03

    Work without a target

    With requirements in flux, the team didn't know what to build next. Social login was already built, and might have to go.

    02 · What I did

    Turning a vague pivot into decisions the client could make

    Alongside leading the front end, I acted as product owner: planning, documentation, acceptance criteria and sign-offs, because that was the gap holding everyone up.

    STEP 1

    Research before building

    We couldn't scope what nobody on the team understood yet.

    With the analysts, I researched the Web3 landscape and presented it to the client as slides they could follow. That covered the technology, and how competing crypto sites were funding and launching their own coins.

    Asking the people who'd use it

    About 15 conversations with crypto and NFT users and crypto-news readers.

    Crypto news sites were still rare, so we went to the people already living in this space: traders, NFT collectors and regular readers of crypto news. I asked what they wanted to see, how they followed news today, and how they expected to sign in.

    The pushback: going fully Web3 mid-build meant scope creep and a blown timeline, built on skills we didn't have yet. I said that plainly, and instead of just refusing, I offered option C.

    The decision: the client chose option C. A Web2 MVP ships first, and Web3 becomes a separate, tested phase instead of a mid-sprint rewrite.Why: an impact analysis instead of a yes/no let the client choose with the costs visible.

    STEP 8

    User stories with acceptance criteria

    Written so the build and the client agreed on what "done" meant.

    EpicAs someone following crypto, I want to see where in the world the news is happening, so that I can spot activity early and read about it in one place.

    US-01 · Activity heat-map

    As a visitor, I want a world map that shows where crypto news is busiest, so that I can see hotspots at a glance.

    1. Countries with more stories show stronger heat.
    2. The map loads centred and zoomable, with a dark base layer.
    3. If the data fails to load, the map still shows and the error is handled quietly.

    US-02 · Country news

    As a visitor, I want to click a country and read its latest stories, so that I can follow up on a hotspot.

    1. Clicking land opens that country's card with the latest stories.
    2. Each story shows its source and how long ago it was posted.
    3. Clicking the ocean or an unknown area doesn't open an empty card.

    US-03 · Social sign-in

    As a visitor, I want to sign in with an account I already have, so that I can use the global view.

    1. Google, Facebook and Twitter sign-in are offered.
    2. Signed-out visitors can browse the map but not the global view.
    3. Signing out returns to the public map.

    US-03b · Wallet sign-in (after change request)

    As a crypto user, I want to sign in with my wallet, so that my identity matches the decentralised direction.

    1. Social sign-in is removed.
    2. Connecting MetaMask signs the user in with their wallet address.
    3. Built and tested on a local network with test tokens before any real deployment.

    Out of scope for Phase 1

    • Launching a coin or token
    • NFT minting and trading
    • Decentralised posting

    Dependencies

    • Crypto news API
    • Backend with story counts by country
    • Map tiles provider
    • Social sign-in service, then MetaMask

    Traceability. Each requirement traces to its story and the check that proved it. As acting product owner, I checked every story against its acceptance criteria with the developers; the client then signed off at the sprint review demo.

    STEP 9

    A use case for the core flow

    Stories say what a user wants. The use case covers how the system responds, including what goes wrong.

    Use case
    View news for a country
    Primary actor
    Visitor
    Precondition
    Map has loaded with heat data
    Main flow
    1. Visitor clicks a point on the map.
    2. System identifies the country from the coordinates.
    3. System fetches that country's latest stories.
    4. System shows the country card with headlines, sources and times.
    Alternate flows
    1. Click on the ocean: nothing opens.
    2. No stories found: card shows "No data found" instead of an empty list.
    3. Request fails: card closes and the map stays usable.
    STEP 10

    Running delivery and controlling change

    Two-week Scrum sprints on a Kanban board, and a rule for every new request.

    Sprint planningFirst day of each sprint: pick the next stories from the backlog by priority.
    Build on the Kanban boardStories move across the board; I check each one against its acceptance criteria with the developers.
    Sprint reviewLast day: demo to the client, take feedback, gather new or changed requirements.
    RetrospectiveInternal team: what went well, what didn't, what to improve.

    STEP 11

    Designing the interface

    I led the front end, so the screens and their decisions were mine.

    Most newsA lotSomeLittleColour scale borrowed from Snap Map, which our users already knew
    • Snap Map colours on a dark map. Red for the most news, blue for the least.Why: users already read red-to-blue heat from Snapchat, so there's nothing to learn, and a dark background makes the heat stand out.
    • Map first, details on click. The map shows only heat; stories open in a card.Why: the heat answers "where?" and the card answers "what?", so the map never clutters.
    • No empty states. Clicking the ocean does nothing, and a country with no stories says so, because a blank card looks like a bug.
    • Wallet sign-in. Social login in the Phase 1 MVP, MetaMask sign-in in the Phase 2 design.Why: the interviews showed crypto users expected to sign in with their wallet, and social login conflicted with the decentralised direction.
    • Scroll inside the card, page across it. A busy country can have dozens of stories, so the compact card has its own scroll bar and pages 1, 2, 3, and expands for more room. Long lists stay usable without covering the map.

    Behind the map: a Python job pulled crypto RSS feeds every day at 3 a.m., stored the stories in a database, counted them per country and fed the heat layer, so the map was always current without manual work.

    STEP 12

    Designing the whole Phase 2 site around daily reading

    I wireframed every page first, then placed the streaks, NFTs and marketplace inside it. This became the Phase 2 plan the client approved.

    Interviews showed people wanted a reason to come back every day, and play-to-earn games like Axie Infinity had the client asking for something similar. Instead of designing a rewards widget on its own, I laid out the full Phase 2 site: navigation, the news map as the home page, a profile, a marketplace and a community chat, so streaks and badges had a real place on the site, not a floating widget.

    • Profile at the top right, with MetaMask inside it. Streak, badges, NFT avatar and wallet sit behind one button.Why: your wallet is your login, so it belongs with your profile.
    • Marketplace and Community as separate tabs. They're secondary to the map, so they sit beside it and never compete with the news.

    STEP 13

    Testing Web3 before committing to it

    Phase 2 started as a safe trial, not a launch.

    None of us knew Web3 when the client asked, so I learned it in time-boxed spikes. Then I wrote and deployed test smart contracts in Solidity with Truffle, ran them on a local Ganache network with dummy coins, and connected MetaMask for wallet sign-in. That showed the client what Web3 would really take, with no real money or users at risk.

    04 · What I'd do differently

    Agree how change works before the first change

    Set the change process at kickoff.

    We introduced the backlog rule after the first pivot. Agreeing it on day one would have saved the weeks of confusion before it.

    Keep a shared glossary.

    "Web3" meant different things to the client, the analysts and the developers. A one-page glossary would have surfaced that immediately.

    Log decisions as they happen.

    Writing down who decided what, and why, keeps everyone aligned when the contact misses a meeting.

    Agree a Definition of Ready.

    No story enters a sprint without acceptance criteria and a confirmed priority, agreed with the client at kickoff.
